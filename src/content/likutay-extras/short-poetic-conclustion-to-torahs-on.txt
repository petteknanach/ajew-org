# Short Poetic Conclusion to Torahs (Alternate Version)

## Alternate Conclusion Poem

נִשְׁלַם הַדָּבָר
וְנִגְמַר הָעִנְיָן
דִּבְרֵי תוֹרָה
חַיִּים וּקְיָמִים
לְדוֹר וָדוֹר
מִדּוֹר לְדוֹר
יְהִי רָצוֹן
מִלְּפָנֶיךָ ה' אֱלֹקֵינוּ
שֶׁיִּהְיוּ דִּבְרֵי תוֹרָה זֹאת
לְשִׂמְחָה וּלְשָׂשׂוֹן
לְאוֹרָה וּלִישׁוּעָה
לְחֵן וּלְחֶסֶד
לְרַחֲמִים וּלְאַהֲבָה
לְסִלּוּחַ וּלְכַפָּרָה
לְטָהֳרָה וּלְקְדֻשָּׁה
לְהַצְלָחָה וּלְבְרָכָה
לְחַיִּים טוֹבִים
וּלְשָׁלוֹם
לָנוּ וּלְכָל יִשְׂרָאֵל
וְנֹאמַר אָמֵן

יִהְיוּ לְרָצוֹן
אִמְרֵי פִי
וְהֶגְיוֹן לִבִּי
לְפָנֶיךָ
ה' צוּרִי וְגֹאֲלִי

## Translation

Completed is the matter
And finished is the subject
Words of Torah
Living and enduring
From generation to generation
From one generation to another
May it be the will
From before You, Hashem our God
That these words of Torah
Be for joy and gladness
For light and salvation
For grace and kindness
For mercy and love
For forgiveness and atonement
For purity and holiness
For success and blessing
For good life
And for peace
For us and for all Israel
And let us say Amen

May they be acceptable
The words of my mouth
And the meditation of my heart
Before You
Hashem, my Rock and my Redeemer

## Commentary

This alternate version of a conclusion poem combines elements from traditional Jewish liturgy with specific Breslov themes. It can be used as a general conclusion after studying any section of Likutay Moharan.

### Structure and Sources

The poem draws from several traditional sources:

1. **Completion formula** - "Completed is the matter" echoes phrases used at the end of Talmudic tractates
2. **Torah as living** - "Words of Torah, living and enduring" reflects the traditional view of Torah as eternally relevant
3. **Generational transmission** - "From generation to generation" emphasizes the continuity of Torah tradition
4. **Personal prayer** - The second part adapts Psalms 19:15: "May the words of my mouth and the meditation of my heart be acceptable before You, Hashem, my Rock and my Redeemer"

### Breslov Elements

While using traditional language, the poem incorporates distinct Breslov emphases:

1. **Joy** - "For joy and gladness" reflects Rabbi Nachman's central teaching about the importance of serving God with joy
2. **Light** - "For light" connects to the Breslov concept of the tzaddik as one who brings spiritual illumination
3. **Grace and kindness** - Reflects the Breslov emphasis on God's lovingkindness (chesed)
4. **Purity and holiness** - Connects to Breslov practices aimed at spiritual refinement

### Liturgical Context

This poem can be used in several ways:

1. **After individual study** - As a personal conclusion to a study session
2. **In group study** - As a communal recitation after learning together
3. **On completion of a section** - Marking the end of a significant unit of study
4. **As a personal meditation** - Reflecting on the goals and outcomes of Torah study

### Spiritual Intentions

When reciting this poem, one might have several intentions (kavanot):

1. **Acceptance** - That the study be accepted by God
2. **Transformation** - That the study lead to personal change
3. **Implementation** - That the teachings be applied in daily life
4. **Communal benefit** - That the study benefit all Israel
5. **Redemptive power** - That the study contribute to collective redemption

### Connection to Psalms 19

The closing lines from Psalms 19 are particularly significant in a Breslov context. Rabbi Nachman taught extensively about the power of speech (particularly prayer and hitbodedut) and the importance of aligning one's inner thoughts with outward expressions.

By concluding with "May the words of my mouth and the meditation of my heart be acceptable before You," the poem emphasizes:
1. **Integration** - The need for external study to penetrate to inner being
2. **Sincerity** - The importance of authentic, heartfelt engagement with Torah
3. **Alignment** - The goal of having one's speech and thoughts in harmony
4. **Divine acceptance** - The ultimate hope that our spiritual efforts find favor with God

### Practical Use

For contemporary students of Likutay Moharan, this poem provides a structured way to:
1. **Mark completion** - Create a sense of closure after study
2. **Set intentions** - Focus on the desired outcomes of study
3. **Connect to tradition** - Link Breslov study with broader Jewish practice
4. **Internalize teachings** - Move from intellectual understanding to heartfelt integration

The poem serves as a bridge between the specific teachings of Likutay Moharan and the universal goals of Jewish spiritual practice, reminding us that all Torah study ultimately aims at drawing closer to God and contributing to the repair of the world.
